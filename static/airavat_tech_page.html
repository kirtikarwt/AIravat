01 — data pipeline

Every alert begins with 23 forecasts of the same 10 days

Twice a day NCMRWF runs its weather model 23 times, each from slightly different starting conditions. Where the runs agree, the forecast is confident; where they disagree, the outcome is genuinely uncertain.

AIravat stacks those runs on top of 30 years of local climate, a 42-year regional history and a 30 m terrain map, all placed on one global mesh of 40,962 near-equal cells.

Where the forecast is extreme, AIravat marks a danger zone: a 640 × 640 km area that is sent on to the 5 km model. Everywhere else, the 12 km forecast is detailed enough.

02 — anomaly fingerprint

Every hazard has its own fingerprint

A cyclone, a cloudburst and a heatwave can all score "extreme". What separates them is the shape of the 36 numbers: deep low pressure and spin for a cyclone, a moisture river and steep terrain for a cloudburst, sinking air and dry soil for a heatwave.

The flower on the right is the fingerprint of the Bay of Bengal cyclone on Day 5 of the forecast (illustrative values). Each petal is one feature; the longer it is, the further that feature is from normal. The dashed ring shows an ordinary day at the same place.

Because every zone is compared with its own 30-year climate, a normal monsoon day in Cherrapunji does not trigger an alert, while a rare downpour in Rajasthan does.

When a fingerprint breaks

How a normal forecast becomes a tracked danger zone.

  • Unusual enoughA mesh cell becomes a candidate when "how unusual" reaches 0.65 and the worst runs lean towards the extreme tail.
  • Grouped, not scatteredCandidates within 200 km of each other are joined into one weather system, so one cyclone is one alert, not fifty.
  • Followed through timeEach system is linked from Day 3 to Day 10, so officers see it form, strengthen and make landfall as one story.
  • Boxed for detailA 640 × 640 km box around each system goes to the 5 km model. One box covers about 12% of India's area, so a typical cycle with one or two boxes zooms into 12–25% of the country instead of all of it.
  • Severity decides who hears firstAdvisory and Watch go to the dashboard; every Warning and Emergency waits for a forecaster's approval, then goes out as a CAP alert to NDMA, the state SDMA and the district control room.

03 — xplainability

Every alert comes with its reasons

A district collector ordering an evacuation needs to know why. AIravat's alert score is not a black box: it is three ingredients added together, each one something a forecaster can check.

How unusual the forecast is for this place and season, whether the worst runs are record-breaking, and the share of runs that cross the danger level.

The score is a guide. The alert level itself comes from clear rules on the same three numbers, so the same forecast always gets the same alert.

04 — mathworks

Why ordinary AI blurs the storm away

Most AI models that sharpen a forecast are trained to be "right on average". When many storms are possible, the average of all of them is a smooth, weak blur, and the peak that floods a village disappears.

The chart shows it in numbers. Real weather keeps energy at small sizes (the navy line, following Kolmogorov's law). The typical AI (orange) loses almost all of it below 24 km.

AIravat's diffusion model draws one possible storm at a time instead of averaging, so its line (blue) stays on top of reality, and the peaks survive.

From noise to forecast in 35 steps

The diffusion model starts from pure noise and removes a little of it at each step, guided by the 12 km forecast and the terrain. After every step the physics check nudges the picture back onto the laws of air, water and energy. (Illustration of the process.)

Six equations, and what each one buys us

The maths behind the dashboard, each paired with the problem it solves.

EquationFormulaWhat it buys us
Extreme Forecast IndexLalaurette, 2003
How unusual the forecast is for this place and date, computed exactly from the 23 runs with no approximation.
Shift of Tails
Keeps rising after EFI saturates, so record-breaking events stand out from merely extreme ones.
Score matching
Trains the diffusion model to remove noise, which lets it draw real storms instead of averages.
Guidance
Pushes each sample towards the extreme the 12 km forecast supports (w = 2.0), without inventing rain.
Mass continuity
Air cannot pile up from nowhere: winds that converge must rise.
Moisture balance
Rain must be paid for by incoming moisture. This removes "phantom cloudbursts".

One model, two halves

A fast global tracker decides where to look; a careful local model decides what it looks like.

Stage 1 · Spherical GNN tracker

Where is the danger, and where is it going?

  • Icosahedral mesh, level 6: 40,962 nearly equal cells, no stretched cells at the poles.
  • 16 layers of message passing between neighbouring cells; rotation-aware, so a cyclone looks the same anywhere.
  • Exact EFI and SOT on every cell, then 200 km clustering and Day 3 → 10 tracking.
  • Output: a handful of 640 × 640 km danger zones.

Stage 2 · Conditional diffusion

What does it look like at 5 km?

  • U-Net with 4 resolution levels (128 → 1024 channels) and attention to the 12 km fields.
  • 23 input channels: the 8 weather fields being generated, the same 8 fields from the 12 km forecast, and 7 terrain maps.
  • 35-step EDM sampler, about 2 s per zone per version (target), 30 versions per zone.
  • Physics projection after every step; guidance w = 2.0 for extremes.
